SB 79 is law. Now read your parcel.

California's transit housing law took effect July 1, 2026, and made roughly 448,000 units of housing legal in the City of Los Angeles overnight — with a million more queued behind a phase-in that runs to 2030. It does not force anything to be built. It changes what is legal to propose, which is how land gets repriced.

By Michael Abraham · AMRE at Compass August 21, 2026 9 min read

Senate Bill 79 — the Abundant and Affordable Homes Near Transit Act — was signed in October 2025 and took effect on July 1, 2026. It overrides local zoning to allow mid-rise apartment buildings of roughly four to nine stories within a half mile of major train stations and rapid bus stops. Los Angeles is the single largest beneficiary, and also the city that has done the most to slow it down.

Key takeaways
  • It sets a floor, not a mandate. A city can no longer reject housing solely for exceeding local height or density limits near qualifying transit.
  • It is narrower than the headlines. Eight counties, under 1% of transit stops statewide — roughly 150 stops inside the City of LA.
  • LA took the slowest lane available. Two ordinances effective June 30, 2026 push full implementation to 2030 and exclude HPOZs entirely.
  • Occupied RSO buildings are not the play. Demolition is barred where a building was rent-controlled and tenant-occupied within the last seven years.
  • The Westside is genuinely in play. About 100,000 units of capacity now, another 60,000 in 2031 — and Santa Monica just upzoned 212 R1 parcels to slow it down.

What the law actually does.

SB 79 sorts transit into tiers by service quality, then sets a height allowance that steps down with distance from the station. Adjacency to a heavy-rail stop buys the most; a half mile from a light-rail stop buys the least. Anything below Tier 2 does not qualify at all.

Every qualifying project must include affordable units — 7% at extremely low income, 10% at very low, or 13% at low. Where a local rule demands more, the local rule wins.

SB 79 does not force anything to be built. It changes what is legal to propose — which is how land gets repriced.

The protection most investors read wrong.

SB 79 is not a demolition tool. Buildings that were rent-controlled and occupied by tenants at any point in the previous seven years cannot be demolished under the law. In the rare permitted replacement, tenants receive moving assistance, three months of rent, subsidized interim housing, and a right to return at an affordable rent.

The practical translation for anyone underwriting LA multifamily: an occupied RSO building bought on a demolition thesis is a thesis the statute forbids. The parcels that actually work are vacant lots, under-built commercial sites, and non-RSO properties inside the rings — the same discipline we apply to any value-add underwrite.

What Los Angeles actually adopted.

This is where the story turns local. The City Council adopted two implementing ordinances on June 23, 2026 — the Low-Rise Ordinance (No. 188967) and the Phased Implementation Ordinance (No. 188968) — effective June 30, one day ahead of the state law.

Together they take what housing advocates called the most restrictive approach available: full implementation deferred to 2030, the statute's 50%-capacity provision used to temporarily exempt sites, industrial employment hubs permanently excluded, and Historic Preservation Overlay Zones carved out entirely. The city's chosen mapping extends Corridor Transition incentives to single-family and lower-density parcels inside the half-mile buffers.

How much capacity, and where.

Parcel-level analysis of the City of Los Angeles puts the eventual number at about 1.46 million units across 17,929 acres — roughly 5% of the city, and nearly 10,000 acres of it previously zoned single-family. Over 100,000 parcels screen as viable for redevelopment. The San Fernando Valley carries the largest share; the Westside is second and, on a price-per-foot basis, the most consequential.

The Westside case study: Santa Monica.

On July 16, 2026, the Santa Monica City Council voted 5–1 to upzone roughly 212 single-family (R1) parcels near the Metro E Line's Expo/Bundy station — instead of the multifamily properties staff had recommended. The logic is procedural, not ideological: the city needed 33% of the 381 parcels in that TOD zone, a 52-parcel threshold, to meet density standards in order to qualify the zone for an SB 79 exclusion.

On a typical 5,500-square-foot R1 lot in that zone, SB 79 standards could support roughly five units. Meanwhile Culver City allowed more housing than the state required, San Francisco phased its sites out to 2032, Burbank floated modifying bus routes to shrink the law's reach, and San Jose sought exemptions across thousands of acres.

A council trying to avoid statewide upzoning voted to upzone single-family homes in order to do it.

What this means for your position.

Policy analysis is only useful if it changes a decision. Here is how SB 79 reads from four different sides of the table.

01

If you own near a station

Your parcel may now carry a development-value floor it did not have in June. No developer is calling tomorrow — but the buyer pool widens from end-users to small builders, and that competition is what moves price. Confirm eligibility in ZIMAS and the City's SB 79 StoryMap before assuming either way.

02

If you are buying

A house a half mile from Expo/Bundy and a house a mile away are now different asset classes with different long-term risk. Ask two questions on every offer: is this parcel eligible, and what is legal next door? Sightlines and parking are the near-term variables; land value is the long-term one. Our buyer's roadmap covers where that check belongs in the process.

03

If you invest in small multifamily

SB 79 stacks on the tools already in play — state density bonus, ED1, ADU and condo-conversion paths. The winning profile is an under-built, non-RSO parcel inside a Tier 1 or Tier 2 ring. The losing profile is an occupied rent-controlled building bought on a demolition thesis the statute forbids.

04

If you are selling in 2026–27

LA's phase-in to 2030 creates a real gap between what the headlines say is legal and what is permittable at your address today. That gap is negotiating leverage — in either direction, depending on which side of it you are standing on. Pricing it correctly means pricing the entitlement, not the rumor.

What we are watching next.

Four things will determine whether the headline numbers become buildings. HCD compliance determinations on the LA, San Francisco, San Jose and San Diego exclusion ordinances, each with a 90-day review window extendable by 30. Litigation — YIMBY Law's suits are the fastest route to testing whether LA's 2030 phase-in survives. Rapid-bus tier designations, since whether corridors like Venice Blvd hold Tier 2 status decides how much of the Westside is truly in play. And the alternative plans cities file for the 7th Housing Element cycle, where density can be moved but not meaningfully reduced.

Common Questions

SB 79, answered.

Does SB 79 mean a nine-story building can go up next to my house?

Only if your parcel is directly adjacent to a Tier 1 station — in Los Angeles that essentially means the Metro B or D Line. Most eligible parcels fall in the five-to-six story range, and in the City of Los Angeles many are deferred until 2030 under the phased implementation ordinance adopted in June 2026.

How do I find out whether my Los Angeles property is eligible?

Check your parcel in ZIMAS and against the City Planning SB 79 eligibility StoryMap. Eligibility depends on the tier of the nearest qualifying transit stop and your distance from it — adjacent, within a quarter mile, or between a quarter and a half mile. Historic Preservation Overlay Zone parcels are excluded in the City of Los Angeles.

Will SB 79 raise or lower my property value?

Both effects are real and they work in opposite directions. Added development potential tends to raise the land value of an eligible parcel, because the buyer pool widens from end users to small builders. Proximity to new mid-rise construction can pressure the premium a single-family home commands. Which effect dominates depends on the specific parcel — its size, its tier, its distance from the station — not on the neighbourhood as a whole.

Can a rent-controlled building be demolished for an SB 79 project?

No. SB 79 prohibits demolition of buildings that were rent-controlled and occupied by tenants at any point within the previous seven years. In the rare cases where replacement is permitted, tenants are entitled to moving assistance, three months of rent, subsidized interim housing, and a right to return at an affordable rent. Occupied RSO buildings are not an SB 79 redevelopment play.

What affordable housing does SB 79 require?

Every qualifying project must include either 7 percent of units at extremely low income, 10 percent at very low income, or 13 percent at low income. Where local rules require a higher share of affordable units, the local requirement prevails.

When does SB 79 actually affect Los Angeles?

The state law took effect July 1, 2026. The City of Los Angeles adopted two implementing ordinances on June 23, 2026, effective June 30, 2026, which use the statute’s exclusion provisions to phase full implementation through 2030. Roughly 448,260 units of capacity are legal now, with about 1,007,890 more following in the 2031 RHNA cycle.
