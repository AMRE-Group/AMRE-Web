Rockets, reels, and the same zip code.

Entertainment and aerospace have shared Los Angeles' coastline — and its payroll — for a hundred years. In the South Bay, that pattern is repeating: SpaceX, Mattel and a legacy aerospace bench are doing to El Segundo, Manhattan Beach and Redondo Beach what Hughes and Douglas did a century ago.

Los Angeles didn't become a tech town in 2012, when people started calling Playa Vista "Silicon Beach." It didn't become an aerospace town in 2002, when a startup called SpaceX leased a warehouse in Hawthorne. It has been an entertainment town and an engineering town, on the same coastline, since the 1920s — and every few decades, the two businesses collide again and rewrite the map of who can afford to live where. The South Bay is where that collision is happening now, and it shows up in the comps, not just the headlines.

Key takeaways
  • The pattern: Movies, aerospace and now software have shared the same handful of LA zip codes for a century — not by coincidence, but because they draw the same capital and the same engineers.
  • The place: El Segundo, Hawthorne and Redondo Beach have been "Aerospace Alley" since 1939. SpaceX, Mattel and Beyond Meat now sit inside that same few square miles.
  • The appreciation: Manhattan Beach's Zillow home-value index sits near $3.29M, up 9.8% in the past year alone — on top of a decade of coastal LA gains that have consistently outpaced the county.
  • The supply problem: These are largely built-out beach cities. New inventory mostly means teardown-and-rebuild, not new subdivisions — which is its own story for value.
  • The tax: Measure ULA now applies at $5.4M (4%) and $10.9M (5.5%) in 2026 — and Redondo Beach's median single-family sale price just crossed the lower threshold.
  • The data: per Compass's Q2 2026 SoCal Quarterly Market Insights Report, El Segundo single-family sales doubled year over year while Manhattan Beach saw fewer sales at higher prices — a classic tight-inventory signature.

A hundred years of the same trade.

Start with the geography, because it explains everything that follows. Douglas Aircraft set up shop in Santa Monica in 1921, a short drive from where silent-film crews were already shooting on empty lots. Within a generation, one man was running both businesses out of the same stretch of coastline: Howard Hughes owned RKO Pictures — the studio lot sat in Hollywood — while simultaneously running Hughes Aircraft's plants strung from Culver City to Playa Vista and, later, a satellite and electronics campus in El Segundo. The same fortune financed both.

World War II turned that coincidence into a regional economy. Northrop opened its Hawthorne plant in 1939. North American Aviation built fighters and bombers in El Segundo and Inglewood. Hughes Aircraft kept expanding through Culver City and Playa Vista. All of it sat a few miles from where MGM, Warner Bros. and Fox were assembling the modern studio system — close enough that the machinists and optical engineers building fuselages by day were, more than occasionally, the same people rigging practical effects by night. The trades weren't adjacent. They overlapped.

The Cold War is what made the South Bay specifically, rather than the Westside generally, the center of gravity. TRW opened its Space Park campus in Redondo Beach in 1961 — the site is Northrop Grumman's Space Systems headquarters today — building satellites two miles from the pier. The Aerospace Corporation, a federally funded research center, had already set up in El Segundo the year before. By the 1980s, El Segundo, Hawthorne and Redondo Beach were known collectively as "Aerospace Alley," one of the densest concentrations of engineering talent in the country, sitting quietly beside some of the most valuable beach real estate in the state.

LA's identity was never just Hollywood. It has always been entertainment and hard engineering sharing a coastline — and a labor pool.

Same coast, new company badges.

What's changed is the badge on the building, not the underlying pattern. The entertainment names have moved from studio lots to streaming platforms; the aerospace names have moved from Cold War contractors to a reusable-rocket company that just went public at a valuation few IPOs have ever touched. Both industries are still here. Both are still hiring the same kind of person — engineers, editors, designers, people comfortable with both risk and precision — and pointing them at the same coastline.

What a decade of this looks like in dollars.

Employer waves are the story; appreciation is the receipt. Zillow's home-value index for Manhattan Beach — a smoothed measure of the typical home, distinct from the median sold price you'll see in the Q2 data below — sits at roughly $3.29 million as of this August, up 9.8% in the past year alone. That single-year number sits on top of a much longer run: coastal South Bay ZIPs have compounded through multiple cycles — the dot-com bust, 2008, the pandemic run-up — and have consistently come out the other side having outpaced Los Angeles County as a whole. It's the kind of appreciation curve that rewards patience over timing, which is exactly the argument for owning here through a demand wave rather than trying to trade around it.

Built out, not built up.

Here's the part that doesn't show up in an employer headcount table: there is almost nowhere left to build. El Segundo, Manhattan Beach and Hermosa Beach were substantially built out by the 1970s, and their zoning has kept it that way. "New construction" in these cities rarely means a subdivision on open land — it means a 1950s aerospace-boom tract home bought for the lot, demolished, and replaced with a architect-built spec home that can sell for two to three times the surrounding comps. El Segundo's Smoky Hollow district is the exception that proves the rule: a former industrial pocket being rezoned for mixed-use and residential infill, one of the only places in the South Bay where meaningful new supply is actually coming online.

For sellers, that scarcity is the quiet asset underneath everything else in this piece. A new employer wave doesn't just compete for existing homes — in a built-out city, it has nowhere else to go.

The ULA effect nobody prices for.

Every conversation about South Bay appreciation eventually runs into Measure ULA — the city's transfer tax on high-value sales, sometimes called the "mansion tax." The thresholds adjust every year for inflation, and the 2026 numbers matter directly to this market:

4.0%
on the portion of a sale from $5.4M to $10.9M
5.5%
on the portion of a sale above $10.9M

Look back at the table below and the relevance is immediate: Redondo Beach's median single-family sale price is now $6.0 million — already inside the 4% tier. Manhattan Beach's $4.4M median sits just under the threshold today, but its top quartile crosses it routinely. This isn't a hypothetical for a handful of ultra-luxury sellers anymore; it's a line item that ordinary South Bay move-up sellers need to plan around, from timing a sale relative to the annual threshold reset to structuring the transaction correctly from the start. Reading the table
  • El Segundo is absorbing new demand without runaway pricing yet — sales doubled, price up a modest 3.2%. Early-innings.
  • Manhattan Beach shows the classic tight-inventory signature: fewer sales (−26.2%), higher price (+10.0%), much slower (+38.2% days on market). Buyers are competing harder for less.
  • Redondo Beach condos (+22.5% sales) and Torrance condos (+25.0%) are where the "value corridor" thesis shows up in real numbers — the near-in, lower-price alternative to Manhattan Beach for the same commute.
  • Playa Vista condo sales jumped 60% — the clearest overlap point between the Silicon Beach tech cluster and the South Bay aerospace corridor.
  • Not every city is up: Palos Verdes Estates and Rancho Palos Verdes both saw sales and, in RPV's case, price soften — a reminder this is uneven, street by street, not a blanket boom.

Common Questions

The South Bay pattern, explained.

Has the South Bay always been an aerospace hub?

Yes. Northrop opened its Hawthorne plant in 1939, TRW's Space Park (now Northrop Grumman) opened in Redondo Beach in 1961, and The Aerospace Corporation set up in El Segundo in 1960. The area was known as "Aerospace Alley" decades before SpaceX existed.

Did Hollywood and aerospace really overlap?

Yes — most visibly through Howard Hughes, who ran RKO Pictures and Hughes Aircraft at the same time in the 1930s and 40s, with facilities a few miles apart across Playa Vista, Culver City and El Segundo.

Is the SpaceX IPO the only tech-driven story in the South Bay?

No. Mattel's global headquarters and Beyond Meat's headquarters are both in El Segundo, and Northrop Grumman's Space Systems division and The Aerospace Corporation are long-standing anchors in Redondo Beach and El Segundo. SpaceX is the newest and largest chapter, not the only one.

Which South Bay city looks like the best value right now?

Per Compass's Q2 2026 data, Redondo Beach and Torrance both show rising condo sales without the price spikes seen in Manhattan Beach — a near-in alternative for buyers who want the same commute at a lower price point.

Does the LA "mansion tax" (Measure ULA) apply in the South Bay?

Yes, for sales within the City of Los Angeles above the annual threshold. For 2026, that's 4% on the portion of a sale from $5.4M to $10.9M, and 5.5% above $10.9M. Redondo Beach's Q2 2026 median single-family sale price of $6.0M already sits inside that 4% tier, and Manhattan Beach's top quartile crosses it routinely. Thresholds adjust every year for inflation, so timing and structuring a sale matters.

How can I sell an outdated South Bay home without paying for repairs upfront?

Compass Concierge covers the cost of pre-sale improvements — painting, flooring, staging, kitchen and bath updates — with nothing due upfront. The cost is repaid out of proceeds at closing (or when the listing ends), which is especially useful for the South Bay's stock of unrenovated 1950s and 60s aerospace-era homes competing against newer spec rebuilds.
