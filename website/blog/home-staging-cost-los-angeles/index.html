“How much does staging cost?” is usually the second question a Los Angeles seller asks us, right after “what’s my house worth?” The two are connected. Staging is one of the few costs before a sale that can change the price, and in a market where buyers scroll listings photo first, an empty or cluttered living room costs you clicks before it costs you offers.

Here is what staging costs from published data, what the National Association of REALTORS® found it does, and how sellers who list with Compass can have it paid for upfront.

What staging actually does

NAR’s 2025 Profile of Home Staging surveyed agents on both sides of the deal.2 83% of buyers’ agents said staging makes it easier for buyers to visualize the property as a future home. On the selling side, 29% of sellers’ agents reported an increase of 1% to 10% in the dollar value offered compared with similar unstaged homes, and 49% said staging reduced time on market (30% slightly, 19% significantly). Among buyers’ agents, 17% reported a 1% to 5% increase.

Read those numbers for what they are: agents’ reports, not a controlled study. Even so, on a $1.5 million Westside home a 1% lift is $15,000 against a staging bill measured in low thousands.

Which rooms to stage first

Sellers’ agents most often stage the living room (91%), primary bedroom (83%), dining room (69%) and kitchen (68%). Buyers rank the living room (37%), primary bedroom (34%) and kitchen (23%) as the most important rooms to stage.2 If the budget is tight, that is the order: the room in the first listing photo, the room people imagine waking up in, and the room they imagine cooking in.

Vacant or lived-in: why the price moves

A vacant home needs furniture brought in and rented by the month, so the cost climbs with every week on market. An occupied home is usually a consult plus a day of editing what is already there: the example vendor quotes on Thumbtack show vacant staging from $2,200 and occupied staging at $800 for a full day.1 In Los Angeles we see a lot of vacant listings: estates, owners who have already moved, and investors selling a turned unit. Those are the ones where a smaller, sharper scope matters most.

Common Questions

Home staging, answered.

How much does home staging cost in Los Angeles?

Published national figures put a typical staging project at $1,361 to $2,831 (Thumbtack, October 2026), and NAR's 2025 report gives a $1,500 median when sellers use a staging service. Vacant homes cost more because furniture is rented by the month. For a specific Los Angeles home, get two or three local bids; we do that for every listing.

Is home staging worth it?

In NAR's 2025 Profile of Home Staging, 83% of buyers' agents said staging helps buyers visualize the home, 29% of sellers' agents reported offers 1% to 10% higher than similar unstaged homes, and 49% said it reduced time on market. These are agent reports, not guarantees.

Which rooms should I stage?

Start with the living room, primary bedroom and kitchen. NAR's 2025 report found those are the rooms buyers rank most important to stage, and the living room and primary bedroom are the rooms sellers' agents stage most often.

Does Compass pay for staging?

Sellers who list with Compass can apply for Compass Concierge, which fronts the cost of staging and more than 100 other pre-sale services with zero due until closing. Repayment is due at sale, when the listing ends, or after 12 months. Loans are provided by Notable Finance, LLC, subject to approval; fees or interest may apply depending on the state.

Is it cheaper to stage an occupied home?

Usually. An occupied home often needs a consultation and a day of editing existing furniture, while a vacant home needs rented furniture for the length of the listing. One vendor's published quotes on Thumbtack show $800 for a full occupied day and vacant staging from $2,200.
