We don't just sell it. We own it.

Any agent looks good when rates are low and buyers are optimistic. The real test is a market where financing is tight and every property needs a clearer story — and that's where owning, renovating and leasing Los Angeles real estate ourselves changes the advice you get.

By Michael Abraham · AMRE at Compass July 30, 2026 7 min read

A few years ago the market was forgiving. Lower rates, stronger optimism and more aggressive underwriting gave almost every deal a margin for error. Today there is no margin for error — and the gap between an agent who has transacted and an owner who has actually lived with the outcome has never been wider.

Key takeaways
  • Owners, first. We buy, renovate, lease and hold Los Angeles property with our own capital — the same asset class we advise on.
  • Renovation math is real math. An architect's read on scope, permits and structure replaces the guess most walkthroughs rely on.
  • We underwrite like landlords. Vacancy, turnover, deferred maintenance and RSO exposure — not just stated rents.
  • Pricing follows the buyer's lender. A number the market's underwriting supports beats a number a seller hopes for.

Most agents have never owned the other side.

They have never signed a loan document with their own name on it. Never funded a renovation that ran three weeks long. Never carried a vacancy through a soft quarter, or sat across from a tenant, or watched a deferred-maintenance item become a five-figure line in year two.

That's not a character judgment — it's a data problem. An agent who has only ever transacted is working from listing data. An owner is working from consequences. In a forgiving market the difference is cosmetic. In this one, it is the entire job.

Anyone can read a comp. Very few people have paid for the mistake the comp doesn't show.

What ownership experience changes in your deal.

Ownership experience isn't a credential to hang on a wall. It shows up in four specific places, and each one is where transactions in this market are won or lost.

The Los Angeles renovation number most agents guess at.

Here's the clearest example. Two people walk the same tired duplex. One sees a kitchen that "needs updating." The other sees a load-bearing wall, a panel that won't carry the new load, a rear addition that never got permitted, and a scope that quietly doubles once the drywall comes off.

Both write an offer. Only one of them is still right in month four. It's the same discipline behind our investor calculator — the returns are only as honest as the cost line beneath them.

Underwriting LA multifamily like a landlord.

A listing shows stated rents. An owner asks a different set of questions: what actually gets collected, what happens at turnover, what the RSO exposure looks like, what the tenant relationships are, and what it costs to get a unit back to market. Those questions don't appear on a marketing flyer — but they determine whether a deal pencils.

We ask them because we've had to answer them on our own buildings — the same math we walk through in the case for duplex ownership in LA.

Common Questions

Experience, explained.

Why does it matter whether my agent owns real estate?

An agent who has owned property has signed the loan documents, paid the down payment, funded the renovation, filled the vacancy and held through a soft year. That experience changes how they price, what risks they flag before escrow, and how realistic their renovation and rent assumptions are. An agent who has only ever transacted is working from listing data; an owner is working from consequences.

What does an architect's background add to real estate advice?

Michael Abraham spent years at the drafting table before becoming a Realtor. Renovation scope, structural risk, permit paths and what a floor plan can and cannot become are read directly off the property rather than estimated. It turns a walkthrough into a feasibility study, which matters most on value-add and fixer properties.

How does landlord experience change how a multifamily property is underwritten?

Landlord experience means underwriting the rent roll the way an owner does, not the way a listing does — realistic vacancy and turnover costs, tenant relocation and RSO exposure in Los Angeles, deferred maintenance that shows up in year two, and the difference between stated rents and collectible rents. Pro formas that ignore those items overstate returns.

Is experience really more important in a slower market?

Yes. When rates are low and buyers are optimistic, wide margins forgive pricing and execution mistakes. When financing is tighter and buyers are selective, the margin for error narrows. Pricing has to reflect what a buyer's underwriting actually supports, buyer targeting has to be specific, and deals under contract have to be actively managed from diligence to closing.
